# SECURITY.md

Concrete security controls for Monadeum, implementing the mitigations `THREAT-MODEL.md` points to. That document enumerates what can go wrong and why; this one states what specifically prevents it. Where a control is not yet built, that is stated as a known limitation, not implied to already exist.

---

## 1. Wallet security

Each agent has its own ERC-4337 smart account on Monad, per `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 6. Two controls apply specifically to the account itself, independent of anything the application layer does:

- **On-chain spend-limit policy**: every agent's smart account enforces a per-transaction cap, a rolling daily cap, and a call allowlist (Kuru's router, the owner-scoped reallocation contract, the settlement asset, and `approve` on each traded asset, the ERC-20 base a close sells, WETH on the default market; nothing else), and a recipient rule (a `transfer` or `approve` on the settlement asset or on a traded asset must name Kuru's router or the reallocation contract; a traded asset counts 0 against the caps and is never a recipient), per the interface in `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.3 and `contracts/src/IAgentSpendPolicy.sol`. The allowlist, the recipient rule, and the per-transaction cap, summed over every call in one operation, are checked in validation: a transaction outside them is rejected by the account itself before it becomes a valid operation, regardless of what any off-chain component approved. The rolling daily cap is checked in execution, because ERC-7562 forbids reading the current time during validation: a breach reverts, and the operation still pays gas. The x402 facilitator is not an allowlisted target. x402 payments are bounded by a second on-chain control instead, below.
- **On-chain x402 payment bounds**: an agent's x402 payments are EIP-3009 `transferWithAuthorization` signatures the account gives through ERC-1271, which never pass through the spend policy (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.3, "x402 signature path"). They are signed by a dedicated x402 key through `X402PaymentValidator`, a Kernel validator module the owner installs. It accepts a signature only when it is, provably, an authorization from the account to a listed provider pay-to address, for at most `maxValue`, valid for at most `maxValidity` (one hour or less), with a nonce from one of `paymentsPerEpoch` slots for the hour of `validAfter`. Anything else, including a `permit`, a `receiveWithAuthorization`, or an over-cap or unlisted transfer, is refused. Because USDC accepts each nonce once, at most `26 * paymentsPerEpoch * maxValue` can leave the account this way in any 24 hours. That budget is separate from, and adds to, `dailyCap`; neither control counts the other's spend, so nothing is counted twice. The validator never validates a user operation, so the x402 key cannot send transactions.
- **Owner-scoped reallocation only**: the reallocation contract (`contracts/src/AgentReallocation.sol`, `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.5), which replaces the reproduction contract of the superseded mechanism (`EVOLUTION.md` sections 3 and 3.6, labeled `reallocation` in the `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.1 allowlist type), moves capital only between existing accounts with the same owner, only while that owner's signed reallocation policy is in force and names both agents, and only within the reallocation cap, capital floor, and slot ceiling, debiting a source at most once per evaluation. It checks all of these on-chain at execution time, debits only the account that calls it, holds no balance, and creates no accounts. The source's `approve` to it is a counted call under the spend policy, so reallocation volume is also bounded by the source's per-transaction and daily caps. Its operator and admin roles must each be a multisig of at least 2-of-3, never a single externally owned account outside local tests; the contract refuses a role address with no code, and its operator cannot open evaluations closer together than a fixed minimum spacing (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.5).
- **Owner-controlled recovery**: the user's embedded wallet (Privy or Dynamic, per `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 6) is the smart account's owner. The owner can rotate or revoke the agent's session key, or sweep remaining funds out of a compromised agent's account, at any time, without needing the agent's own cooperation or the platform operator's intervention. Revocation is paymaster-sponsored outside the per-agent gas budget (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 7), so neither a spent budget nor an account without MON delays it; only a paymaster outage combined with an account without MON does, and that state is reported, not hidden.

## 2. Agent permissions

Permissions are additive only by explicit, owner-authorized action, never automatically, and never in response to an incoming transfer. Security invariant 2 in `THREAT-MODEL.md` states this directly: receiving a token or NFT never changes an agent's spend limits, allowlist, or session-key scope. This is the direct, concrete answer to the real incident (`THREAT-MODEL.md` scenario B) where an unsolicited NFT transfer was treated as sufficient authorization to expand an agent's transaction-signing capability elsewhere; in this system, there is no code path that reads "received an asset" as an input to a permission decision at all.

A session key is scoped narrowly at issuance: it can invoke Kuru's router, the reallocation contract, the settlement asset, and `approve` on each traded asset, and nothing else, within the smart account's own spend limits (section 1). It cannot sign for the account through ERC-1271 at all, because the spend policy's signature check always fails. **Kernel's skip-signature flag is never set on the spend policy, for any agent**: a Monad mainnet fork showed that with it, a session key's signature makes USDC grant a `permit` of the whole balance to any spender (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.3, fact 5). x402 payments are signed by a separate x402 key, scoped by `X402PaymentValidator` to bounded payments to listed providers (section 1), and that key can do nothing else. Each key holds exactly one power: the trading key cannot pay x402, and the x402 key cannot trade or send a transaction. Changing the x402 payee list or bounds is an owner reinstall through the root validator; the validator refuses any configuration write that Kernel's own state does not show as the owner's install or uninstall. Scope is never widened by the agent's own request; widening a scope is a user action taken through the dashboard, mediated by the same WalletsModule that issued the key in the first place. The same holds for the reallocation policy: only the owner's root wallet signs it, it can only tighten the protocol bounds, and no agent can propose a change to it.

## 3. Smart contract security

Everything in this document that depends on "the smart account rejects out-of-policy transactions" depends on that validator module's implementation being correct. This document states the requirement rather than the proof:

- Contracts are built with Foundry, with unit, integration, fuzz, and invariant tests, per the brief's own testing requirements, before any testnet deployment.
- The specific invariants that must hold under fuzzing include, at minimum, every numbered invariant in `THREAT-MODEL.md` section 7, expressed as executable invariant tests, not just prose: spend-limit rejection, capital conservation across reallocation (invariant 3), the per-evaluation cap and capital floor on a source (invariant 4), the slot ceiling on a sink (invariant 12), and no capital crossing an owner boundary (invariant 13) are the most directly testable as contract-level invariants. Parent-capital-never-below-principal was the corresponding invariant of the superseded reproduction contract.
- An external audit is a gate before mainnet, not an optional step; `deploy-monad-mainnet` checklist practice applies here directly. No mainnet deployment carrying real user capital happens before an audit is complete and its findings are resolved or explicitly accepted as residual risk.
- Testnet deployments of the contracts below (`contracts/script/DeployTestnet.s.sol`, chain 10143 only) carry testnet assets only; they are not deployments carrying real user-controlled capital, and the gates below are unchanged for any deployment that would.
- The spend-policy contract (`contracts/src/AgentSpendPolicy.sol`) is flagged for external audit with a stricter gate: no deployment carrying real user-controlled capital, on testnet or mainnet, happens before that audit. The Monad testnet deployment of 2026-10-11 (section 13) holds testnet assets only, which carry no real value, so it does not cross this gate. Its tests express `THREAT-MODEL.md` invariant 1 as Foundry invariants over fuzzed operation sequences: no accepted operation counts more than `perTxCap`, no 24-hour period holds more than `dailyCap` of accepted spend, and no call outside the configured allowlist, or to the policy or the account itself, is accepted at any amount. Its audit package (`contracts/audit/README.md`, issue #46) holds the scope, the integration assumptions, the invariants mapped to tests, the coverage report, the static-analysis triage, the known limitations, and a draft request for proposal; no auditor is engaged yet.
- `X402PaymentValidator` (`contracts/src/X402PaymentValidator.sol`), which bounds the account's x402 payment signatures (section 1), is under the same gate and in the same audit package (`contracts/audit/README.md` section 12). Its tests express `THREAT-MODEL.md` invariant 17 as Foundry invariants against a fully compromised x402 key: every outflow goes to a listed payee within `maxValue`, no 24-hour period exceeds the rate bound, no `permit` is ever approved, and direct calls from the account never widen the configuration. A Monad mainnet fork test runs it against the real USDC and Kernel v3.1 contracts.
- The owner-scoped reallocation contract specifically is the one most directly responsible for the capital-conservation and owner-boundary invariants; it receives disproportionate audit and fuzz-testing attention relative to its code size, because a bug there is the one place this document's core financial guarantees (`THREAT-MODEL.md` invariants 3 and 13) could actually be violated on-chain. Its fuzz targets include adversarial sequences: repeated calls inside one evaluation, a source and sink with different owners, a revoked or replaced policy, a retired agent as source or sink, and amounts at each bound. Its audit package is section 13 of `contracts/audit/README.md`: `THREAT-MODEL.md` invariants 3, 4, 12, 13, and 15 mapped to tests, coverage, the Slither and Aderyn triage, and its limitations, including operator trust and the minimum evaluation spacing.
- The reallocation contract (`contracts/src/AgentReallocation.sol`, issue #31) is flagged for external audit with the same stricter gate as the spend policy: no deployment carrying real user-controlled capital, on testnet or mainnet, happens before that audit. The audit scope includes its operator registry and the trust it places in that role (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.5), its EIP-712 policy signatures, and its interaction with the spend policy. Its Foundry tests express `THREAT-MODEL.md` invariants 3, 4, 12, 13, and 15 as invariants over the fuzzed sequences above: total and per-owner capital are conserved, no source loses more than the cap in one evaluation or ends below the floor, no source is debited twice in one evaluation, no sink ends above the ceiling, no move crosses owners or touches a retired or unregistered agent, and retirement moves no capital. An independent model of every rule must agree with the contract on every outcome, and each rule was removed in turn to confirm the tests fail without it.

## 4. AI security: separating proposal from authorization

This is the single most load-bearing control in the entire system, and it is structural, not a configuration setting. An agent's LLM reasoning step produces a **proposal**: an intended action, with a stated rationale. That proposal is evaluated by the **Policy/Risk Engine**, a deterministic, non-LLM component, against the agent's actual configured limits, asset allowlist, and the structural shape of the transaction itself (destination, amount, asset), independent of whatever justification the LLM attached to it. Only an approved proposal reaches the **execution** layer, and even there, the on-chain smart account's own validator (section 1) is a second, independent check that does not trust the Policy/Risk Engine's correctness either.

This three-stage separation exists because of a specific, repeated pattern in the two real incidents `THREAT-MODEL.md` documents: in both, the LLM's own judgment was the only safeguard, and in both, an attacker successfully argued the LLM into believing an unauthorized action was authorized. Concretely, the Policy/Risk Engine never evaluates "does this proposal sound legitimate"; it evaluates "is this destination in the allowlist, is this amount within the cap, is this asset in the agent's configured universe," questions with deterministic, structural answers that no amount of persuasive text in a prompt can change.

**Prompt injection posture**: every piece of text that reaches an agent's LLM context from outside the platform's own code, an x402 API response body, an on-chain string field, a future agent-to-agent message, is treated as untrusted input by default, following both Monad's own stated guidance ("every attacker-influenced value you return, store, or log is live input") and the industry pattern of scanning untrusted content that enters a model's context window for adversarial instructions before it is used. This is a defense-in-depth measure on top of, not a substitute for, the structural policy-engine check above; the policy engine's guarantee does not depend on prompt-injection defenses working, because it never trusts the LLM's conclusions in the first place, only the injection-scanning layer is what keeps an agent's actual decision quality from being degraded by manipulated input, which matters for the fitness metric even when it cannot matter for fund safety.

## 5. x402 security

- **Provider allowlist, not open discovery**, for the MVP: the agent worker's x402 client is restricted to two named, manually vetted providers, Nansen and DeFiLlama, closing off the discovery-layer manipulation attack `THREAT-MODEL.md` scenario C describes by construction rather than by runtime scoring. Expanding this list is a deliberate, reviewed action, not something an agent's own genome or reasoning can do on its own.
- **Per-resource idempotency tracking on the client side**, never assumed from the provider or facilitator: the x402 client records which resource identifiers it has already paid for in the current window and treats a repeated or duplicate grant as an anomaly to log, not to silently accept, mitigating the replay pattern documented against x402 generally.
- **No caching of paid responses at any intermediary the platform controls**, explicit `no-store` discipline on every HTTP path that touches a paid x402 response, directly closing the cache-leakage pattern documented against x402's HTTP-layer implementation.
- **On-chain bound under the client**: the client is not the enforcement point for what a payment can cost. Every authorization it obtains must also pass `X402PaymentValidator` (section 1): listed pay-to address, at most `maxValue`, a validity window of at most `maxValidity`, and a free per-hour slot. A slot is taken from the payment log before signing, so two purchases never share a nonce; a reused slot is refused by USDC rather than paid twice, and an hour whose slots are used up ends further purchases that hour as a failed call.
- **Every x402 payment is logged with its outcome**: provider, price, response summary, and whether the response changed the agent's subsequent decision. This is both a cost-accounting requirement (feeds the fitness function in `EVOLUTION.md`) and a security-monitoring input (section 9 below).

## 6. External API security

Every external response, from an x402 provider or otherwise, is validated against an expected schema before any part of it is used in an agent's reasoning or stored. A malformed or unexpected response is treated as a failed call (see section 8), not partially trusted. This applies even to the two allowlisted providers: allowlisting establishes that a provider was chosen deliberately, not that every individual response from it is trustworthy content rather than untrusted input.

The optional Nansen Smart Money signal (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 8.1) follows the same rule on the API key path: the body is size-limited and schema-validated, only finite numbers are read, and the output is one number in [-1, 1] that can change an entry condition but never a position size. The key is read from `NANSEN_API_KEY` in the environment only, is never logged, and is dropped from error text. Any failure means no signal, never a default.

**Tool poisoning, considered separately.** A malicious API response (section 5 and the paragraph above) attacks the data a tool returns. Tool poisoning attacks the tool itself: the description, parameter schema, or metadata an agent reads to decide *which* tool to call and *how*, so the agent misuses a legitimate tool, for example the x402 client or the trading interface, without any direct bypass of a financial permission. It matters most where tool definitions come from a third party at runtime, such as an open tool registry or a provider's self-description. The MVP closes that surface by construction rather than by runtime scanning, with two rules. First, every tool definition an agent's LLM sees is static and versioned in this repository, never loaded from a provider, registry, or discovery service at runtime. Second, descriptive text a provider supplies alongside a payment request or a response never enters the LLM context; only the validated, typed fields do. A poisoned tool could still degrade decision quality, but it cannot move capital outside policy, because a tool call is only ever a proposal to the Policy/Risk Engine (section 4). Admitting third-party tools, for example external MCP servers or an open x402 discovery layer, reopens this surface and requires revisiting this paragraph first.

## 7. Transaction security

- **Structural validation before signing**: the Policy/Risk Engine checks that a proposed transaction's asset exists in the agent's allowed universe, its amount is within configured bounds, and its destination is in the allowlist, independent of the LLM's stated reasoning. This is the direct mitigation for `THREAT-MODEL.md` scenario F, a hallucinated or malformed transaction proposal, a risk category this project found no established external prior art for and is defending against with a first-principles structural check rather than a borrowed pattern.
- **Idempotency keyed to (agent ID, tick sequence number)** on every job the agent worker processes, so a worker crash and retry cannot double-submit a trade or a payment; see `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 14.
- **Gas-limit awareness**: Monad charges gas based on the submitted gas limit, not gas actually used, a documented, non-obvious difference from Ethereum. The transaction-building layer estimates gas limits deliberately rather than padding generously "for safety," since over-padding directly costs more MON on Monad in a way it would not on Ethereum.
- **Funding step** (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 6.1, `THREAT-MODEL.md` scenario H and invariant 16): the API sets an on-ramp session's destination to the authenticated user's own embedded wallet and locks the asset to USDC on Monad; the client cannot supply either. The on-ramp opens only from inside the app, and the product never sends funding links by email or message. A balance is credited only from an observed on-chain transfer of the canonical USDC contract; the vendor's webhook is verified and used for progress display only. The allocation authorization is built by the API for an agent smart account address the API has derived and checked belongs to the user, and the relayer submits exactly what the user signed. Relayed allocations are capped per user per day (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 7). As implemented on testnet (`apps/api`): the sender is the owner wallet on the agent's record and the recipient the account WalletsModule recorded as deployed, which must have code on chain, neither taken from the request; the client sends back only the nonce and the signature, and the signature must recover the owner over the stored fields, so it cannot be applied to another recipient or amount; the nonce is random, 32 bytes, stored once, and claimed once, and the token itself accepts it once; the window is short (600 seconds by default); each transfer is capped (`API_FUNDING_MAX_PER_TRANSFER`), and the per-user budget is claimed atomically with the submission. The relayer simulates the call before sending, refuses when it cannot pay the gas limit, and records the transaction hash before broadcasting. The signing screen states the agent's name, the amount in USDC, the sender, and the recipient, derived from the typed data the wallet will sign, and tells the owner not to sign if they disagree with the request.

## 8. Fail-closed behavior

Stated once, applied everywhere: any state where the system cannot determine whether a proposed action is within policy does not execute the action. A timed-out RPC call, a malformed API response, an ambiguous account state, a worker that crashed mid-decision, none of these are treated as "probably fine, proceed." Each is treated as a failed tick: no action taken, logged, retried on the next scheduled cycle. This is `THREAT-MODEL.md` invariant 10 restated as an operating rule rather than a proof obligation. On the x402 path, an account without `X402PaymentValidator` installed cannot pay at all, and a purchase with no free slot in the current hour, or with terms the validator would refuse, is never signed: it is a failed call (section 5), not a reason to sign through any other key or to set Kernel's skip-signature flag.

## 9. Emergency controls and circuit breakers

A layered-cap pattern, matching the production precedent found in research (Coinbase's Agentic Wallets enforces spending policy at the smart-contract level specifically so an agent cannot exceed its limit even if application logic is compromised):

1. **Per-transaction and per-day caps, on-chain, per agent** (section 1). No application bug or compromised worker process can exceed these, because the chain itself rejects the transaction.
2. **A session-level budget enforced by the Policy/Risk Engine**, tighter than the on-chain cap (deployment-wide on testnet today, not per agent; section 13), evaluated before a transaction is even submitted. This catches problems earlier than the on-chain layer, at the cost of being bypassable if the application layer itself is compromised, which is exactly why layer 1 exists independently.
3. **An aggregate, population-level circuit breaker**, tracking total capital at risk across all live agents and the aggregate capital moved by reallocation per evaluation. If either crosses a configured threshold, this is the platform-wide equivalent of a kill switch: new agent ticks stop being scheduled, and reallocation is paused, until a human reviews and clears the condition. This is the control that specifically answers `THREAT-MODEL.md` scenario D, capital drifting toward a strategy whose risk the fitness metric does not capture; no single agent's individual spend limit catches a slow, population-wide drift toward a bad strategy, only an aggregate view can. (Under the superseded reproduction mechanism the second signal was the aggregate reproduction rate.)
4. **Manual, immediate per-agent stop**: the user can retire any agent from the dashboard at any time, which revokes its session key immediately (section 1); the operator can do the same for any agent platform-wide in an incident.

### Bulk recovery hold

The owner path in section 1 (revoke, rotate, or sweep) stays immediate for one agent. It does not stay immediate when one root wallet would touch many agents in a short window. A phished or otherwise compromised root credential could otherwise revoke or sweep every agent that owner controls in one session. That is the same blast-radius shape as layer 3 above, with credential compromise in place of a degenerate strategy. The application hold below is implemented in `libs/recovery`. Chain-level enforcement of the same rule still belongs to the wallet and validator work (issues #24 and #25). The thresholds are proposed defaults, not a claim that these exact numbers have been measured.

- Count the distinct agent accounts a root wallet has revoked or swept during the last 60 minutes.
- An action that names exactly one agent executes immediately when the count, including that agent, is still 3 or lower.
- An action that names two or more agents is held. It does not execute.
- An action that names one agent, but would push the distinct-agent count above 3 inside the window, is held. It does not execute.
- A held action notifies the user and lists the agents it would affect. Releasing it takes a second explicit confirmation that restates how many agents are affected, followed by a 15 minute delay, and only then execution.
- A legitimate owner who wants to retire every agent can still do it. They confirm the count and wait 15 minutes. They do not get a path that skips the hold.
- A single revoke or sweep does not wait. Revoking agent A, and revoking agent B an hour later, both execute at once. Revoking A, then B, then C inside the same hour still executes. The request that would touch a fourth distinct agent, or any request that names two agents at once, waits.

## 10. Operating modes

The product runs in one of three modes. This section assembles the controls `BRIEF.md` requires for each mode and points at the document that already specifies the mechanism. It does not add a control.

### Simulation

No real money moves. Inputs are historical or synthetic. A run with a given seed is deterministic. Market conditions, costs, and strategy parameters are configurable.

- No real money, and no wallets or chain state: `BRIEF.md` (Simulation engine) and `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 10 (`SimulationModule`).
- Deterministic historical or synthetic markets: `ECONOMICS.md` sections 3 and 7, and `BRIEF.md` (Simulation engine).
- Configurable conditions: `BRIEF.md` (Simulation engine) and the cost inputs in `ECONOMICS.md` section 1.

### Testnet

Testnet uses real testnet assets. It exists to validate the paths mainnet will use. It runs on one VPS the owner controls, per `deploy/README.md` and `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 10 ("Deployment on testnet"), with the local encrypted session key vault of section 11.

- Wallets and smart accounts: `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 6.
- x402: `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 8 and section 5 of this document.
- Reallocation and retirement: `EVOLUTION.md` sections 3 and 6.
- Accounting: `ECONOMICS.md` section 2. Reallocation moves capital that already exists between one owner's agents. It does not create capital, and it never crosses owners.
- Trading: `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 10 (`TradingModule`).
- Policies: sections 1 and 4 of this document.

### Mainnet

Mainnet is opt-in per user and per agent. It is never the default, and unrestricted autonomous mainnet trading is not a mode of this system.

Opt-in is two stored flags, `user.mainnetEnabled` and `agent.mainnetEnabled`. Both default to false. An agent may execute on mainnet only when both are true. Each flag changes only by an explicit action of the root-wallet owner, and that action is stored with its timestamp. Funding an agent, deploying its smart account, or finishing a testnet run does not set either flag. A missing flag is not mainnet.

`BRIEF.md` requires the following controls on mainnet. Each one is already specified elsewhere:

- Spending limits: section 1 (per-transaction cap and rolling daily cap) and the tighter session budget in section 9.
- Position limits: `EVOLUTION.md` section 1, the protocol ceiling on `riskTolerance`.
- Loss limits: the rolling daily spend cap in section 1, the population-level capital-at-risk circuit breaker in section 9, and the minimum viable capital floor in `EVOLUTION.md` section 6. Those three are the loss controls the document set actually specifies. There is no separate maximum-drawdown parameter beside them.
- API budgets: `informationBudget` in `EVOLUTION.md` section 1, and the per-tick and daily x402 budget in `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 3.
- Gas budgets: the per-agent paymaster budget in `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 7. Session-key revocation is sponsored outside it, so a spent budget never blocks the emergency stop; past the budget, other operations pay gas in native MON from the agent's account.
- Reallocation limits: the reallocation cap, capital floor, slot ceiling, and owner policy in `EVOLUTION.md` sections 3.1 and 4. (The superseded reproduction limits, eligibility, `max_child_capital`, and the per-parent descendant cap, are in `EVOLUTION.md` sections 3.6 and 4.)
- A maximum population: the global population cap in `EVOLUTION.md` section 4.
- An allowlist of permitted contracts: section 1 of this document.
- An emergency stop: section 9 of this document (the population circuit breaker, and the immediate per-agent stop).
- Withdrawal controls: `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 3 (a withdrawal back to the user stays inside the user's configured limits) and section 1 of this document (the owner can sweep a compromised agent's account).

## 11. Key management

- **User root keys**: never held by the platform. The embedded wallet vendor (Privy or Dynamic) manages the user's own key material under its own custody model; the platform never has access to the user's root signing key.
- **On-ramp data**: card details and identity documents are collected by the on-ramp vendor inside its own widget (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 6.1). The platform does not receive or store them; it stores only the vendor's order identifier and status. The vendor API key is a secret under the rule below.
- **Allocation relayer key**: the platform relayer that submits users' EIP-3009 allocation authorizations holds MON for gas only, never user funds, and is funded no further than its capped budget. It cannot alter an authorization's recipient or amount, which the user's signature fixes. On testnet it is held in its own instance of the local encrypted file vault (below): vault purpose `relayer`, its own passphrase file and volume, mounted into the API only, created on first boot, refused with `MONADEUM_ENV=production`. Only its address is logged or served (`GET /health`). What a compromise of it gives an attacker: the relayer's MON, and the ability to submit authorizations users already signed, each of which moves only the signed amount to the signed agent account, once; it cannot create an authorization. Mainnet needs the same secrets manager as the session keys before a relayer holds mainnet MON.
- **Agent session keys**: held server-side by agent worker processes, scoped narrowly (section 2), rotated on a schedule and immediately on any suspected compromise, and revocable by the user at any time without needing platform operator action.
- **Agent x402 keys**: one per agent, separate from its trading session key, held under the same `SessionKeyVault` rule, and usable only for the bounded payment signatures of section 1. Revoking one means uninstalling `X402PaymentValidator` from the account, never only invalidating Kernel's nonce: Kernel v3.1's ERC-1271 path does not read the nonce, so an x402 key stays able to sign until the validator is uninstalled (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.3, fact 4). Uninstalling also invalidates every authorization signed earlier and not yet settled, because USDC checks the signature at settlement. Rotation is an uninstall followed by an owner reinstall with the new key.
- **Database**: Postgres (`libs/db`, `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 10) holds no private key, session key, or plaintext bearer token. Platform session tokens are stored only as their SHA-256, and the schema rejects any other value. Session and x402 private keys live behind the `SessionKeyVault` interface, never in Postgres. For production that interface needs a secrets manager, which is not built, so no production environment may hold a live session key until one is.
- **Testnet and local development: the local encrypted vault (owner decision).** On Monad testnet and in local development only, session and x402 keys are held in `FileSessionKeyVault` (`libs/wallets`, `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 10, "Deployment on testnet"): each key sealed with AES-256-GCM under a key derived with scrypt from a passphrase held in a file outside the repository and outside Postgres, files mode 0600 in a 0700 directory, separate vaults and passphrases for session and x402 keys, and no key or passphrase in any log or error. It refuses to open with `MONADEUM_ENV=production`. What it does not protect against: anyone who obtains both the vault files and a passphrase file, for example through a compromise of the VPS, holds those keys until their on-chain revocation completes; and restoring a vault backup brings back keys destroyed after the backup was taken. Both are why every stop completes on chain, never by deleting a key alone. This is accepted for testnet, where accounts hold testnet assets only, and is not acceptable for mainnet.
- **Risk policy operator key**: signs each RiskModule policy version (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.1). A Foundry keystore account (encrypted with the owner's password) on the owner's workstation, distinct from the deployer, the Safe owners, and every key a process holds; it never reaches the VPS, which receives only the signature. The worker pins its address (`WORKER_POLICY_SIGNER`) and runs no tick without a version it signed. What a compromise of it gives an attacker: the power to sign a looser policy, which still needs `monadeum_policy_operator` database access to be appended and stays bounded by every account's on-chain caps and allowlist (section 1).
- **Testnet contract deployer**: a Foundry keystore account (encrypted with the owner's password) on the owner's workstation, holding testnet MON only. Deployment scripts take it with `--account` and never read a private key from a file, an environment variable, or the command line.
- **No private key, of any kind, is ever committed to source control, logged in plaintext, or stored outside the vendor's own key-management infrastructure or a dedicated secrets manager.** This is a hard rule, not a preference, and applies equally to development, testnet, and mainnet environments, with the two testnet and local-development exceptions above: keys encrypted at rest in the local file vault, and the encrypted Foundry keystore of the testnet deployer.

## 12. Monitoring

Every agent action, decision, x402 payment, transaction, trade, error, capital change, fitness change, reallocation, retirement, and reallocation policy change is logged with enough detail to reconstruct what happened without reading raw logs, feeding the dashboard views defined in `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 15. Specific patterns monitored for anomaly, beyond simple logging:

- An agent whose x402 spend pattern changes sharply (for instance, paying the same provider on every tick regardless of whether it changes the agent's decision) is flagged, since this is one of the concrete signals a compromised or degenerate agent could produce.
- Every on-chain x402 settlement from an agent account (USDC's `AuthorizationUsed` and `Transfer` events) is matched against a payment-log entry; a settlement with no entry, or to a pay-to address that is not the logged provider's, is alerted on immediately, since it means an x402 key signed outside the platform's own client. The check exists as `reconcileSettlements` in `libs/x402`, with the alert types `x402-settlement-unmatched` and `x402-settlement-mismatch`; the worker runs it as the repeatable `x402-reconcile` job (`WORKER_X402_RECONCILE_INTERVAL_MS`, default 300000), which logs each alert as an `ALERT` line and checks nothing while the x402 payer is disabled. An agent that uses all of an hour's x402 slots repeatedly is flagged.
- An agent approaching its on-chain spend cap repeatedly without breaching it is flagged as worth reviewing, since it suggests the on-chain cap is doing load-bearing work that the application-layer policy engine's own tighter budget should ideally have caught first. The spend-policy contract's `Spent` event carries each operation's counted amount and the resulting window total, and `windowTotal` can be read at any time, so this signal can come from the chain rather than from the application's own ledger.
- Changes to the reallocation contract's registry (`AgentRegistered`, `PrincipalSet`, `AgentRetired`), its evaluation counter (`EvaluationAdvanced`), and its roles (`OperatorSet`, `AdminTransferred`) are compared with the platform's own records of agent creation, deposits, withdrawals, retirement, and the evaluation schedule. A principal that does not match observed deposits and withdrawals, or evaluations advancing faster than the cadence, is alerted on, since those are the levers a compromised operator key holds (`THREAT-MODEL.md` section 8). The operator and admin addresses are checked to be the deployed multisigs, with the recorded threshold of at least 2-of-3 and the recorded signer set, at deployment (the testnet deployment script refuses a role with a threshold below 2 or fewer than 3 owners, and `contracts/deployments/monad-testnet.json` records the Safe's threshold and owners) and after every `OperatorSet` or `AdminTransferred` event; any change of signers or threshold, or a role moving to another address, is alerted on immediately.
- Reallocations are monitored in aggregate for volume anomalies (section 9, layer 3) and individually against their bounds: a move between different owners, without a policy in force, above the cap, below the floor, above the ceiling, or involving a retired agent should be structurally impossible if the planner and the contract are implemented correctly, and a revert or a ledger record showing one is alerted on immediately.
- A genome whose on-chain hash does not match its off-chain record, or any write to a live agent's genome, is alerted on immediately, since genomes are fixed at creation (`EVOLUTION.md` section 3.1).
- An owner whose capital is repeatedly concentrated at the slot ceiling in one agent is flagged for review, since that is where reallocation amplifies one strategy's tail risk most (`THREAT-MODEL.md` scenario D).

## 13. Known limitations

Stated honestly, matching `THREAT-MODEL.md` section 8's residual-risk posture:

- The on-chain spend-limit validator module described throughout this document is written (`contracts/src/AgentSpendPolicy.sol`, issue #25) and has Foundry unit, fuzz, and invariant tests, but it is not audited. It is deployed on Monad testnet only, at `0xe54512401B8fCD0DCc3f4Aa4929b99F05AbA3f05` (2026-10-11, block 70167444, recorded in `contracts/deployments/monad-testnet.json`), and not on mainnet. Its Kernel v3.1 integration has been checked against Kernel's source, a test double, and the Kernel v3.1 contracts on a fork of Monad testnet, and on 2026-10-11 one account deployment through ZeroDev's bundler on testnet installed it: the indexer recorded the account's deployment and the policy's limits and allowlist events for it. No spending operation validated by it through a bundler has been observed yet (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.3). Every guarantee in sections 1, 4, and 9 that depends on it is provisional until a spending operation runs through a bundler against it and the contract passes the audit gate in section 3. Its full known-limitations list is in `contracts/audit/README.md` section 8.
- Section 1 says an out-of-bounds transaction is rejected before it becomes a valid operation. That holds for the allowlist, the recipient rule, and the per-transaction cap. It may not hold for the rolling daily cap: ERC-7562's validation rules forbid reading the current time during validation, so the interface in `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.3 checks the daily cap in the execution phase, where a breach reverts but still pays gas. Issue #18's testnet run found no native ZeroDev mechanism that changes this: the closest native combination neither bounds batched operations nor rolls, per `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.3, so the rolling daily cap depends entirely on the custom spend-policy contract.
- x402 payments are bounded on-chain by `X402PaymentValidator` (section 1), which is written and tested, including against the real USDC and Kernel v3.1 contracts on a Monad mainnet fork, but not audited. It is deployed on Monad testnet only, at `0xCA918F53FFc7507Bd41602c0bD1ef72558705991` (2026-10-11, block 70167461), and not on mainnet; the testnet runbook leaves it uninstalled on accounts (`deploy/README.md`, open question 3). WalletsModule installs it in the deployment operation when x402 is configured, and the worker's payer stays disabled until a facilitator on Monad is confirmed to accept ERC-1271 (`WORKER_X402_PAYER_ENABLED`, off by default). Until both hold, an agent cannot pay x402 at all, because the trading key cannot sign through ERC-1271 and the payer signs nothing; that is the intended fail-closed state. `maxValue` and `paymentsPerEpoch` are configuration; the defaults, 0.05 USDC and 4 per hour (a 24-hour worst case of 5.20 USDC per agent), are the values the owner decided, with `maxValidity` 300 seconds. Its bound is a rate bound, `26 * paymentsPerEpoch * maxValue` per 24 hours, not an amount window like `dailyCap`, so the deployment must size `paymentsPerEpoch` and `maxValue` so that the product is an acceptable worst case; the RiskModule's information caps remain the tighter working limit. Whether the facilitator used on Monad accepts ERC-1271 signatures for EIP-3009 is not verified (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.3, open question 1); if it does not, the fallback is a separately funded hot balance, which weakens the recipient rule and is not adopted without its own decision. The skip-signature flag on the spend policy remains forbidden: the fork confirmed that USDC accepts ERC-1271 signatures for `permit` and `transferWithAuthorization`, so that flag would let a session key approve or transfer the whole balance to any address.
- The on-chain recipient rule (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.3) stops a compromised session key from paying the settlement asset to an address of its choosing, but only through `check`. It does not see calls that count 0: whether Kuru's router can be made to forward funds to an arbitrary address is a property of the router, not yet checked (issue #20). It does not see x402 payments either; those are restricted to the x402 validator's own payee list, per the item above. Within those limits, a compromised key can still lose funds up to the caps through bad trades or misplace them through a bounded reallocation, but it cannot transfer them to itself directly, and a reallocation it pushes stays inside the owner's agents and the contract's bounds.
- Section 9's layer 2 is deployment-wide, not per agent: the RiskModule's caps come from one signed policy, and the testnet runbook sets them to the largest caps the API lets an owner choose. For an agent whose owner chose lower on-chain caps, only layer 1 binds below the policy's caps: a plan above the agent's per-transaction cap is refused in validation and pays nothing, and one above its remaining daily cap reverts in execution and pays gas. Taking the minimum of the policy and the agent's own limits is the extension `ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.1 describes; the worker does not read `limits(account)` yet (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 11, "Tick runner").
- The reallocation ranking is computed off-chain and is not verifiable on-chain; a corrupted ranking can misdirect capital among one owner's agents within the contract's bounds (`THREAT-MODEL.md` section 8).
- The reallocation contract is written (`contracts/src/AgentReallocation.sol`, issue #31) and has Foundry unit, fuzz, and invariant tests, but it is not audited. It is deployed on Monad testnet only, at `0x4e7500415e81017670a9cFb8763f873EE6694A7f` (2026-10-11, block 70167453), with reallocation off in that deployment, and not on mainnet. It measures capital as the settlement-asset balance, so a sink holding a position in another asset can exceed the slot ceiling in total value; it reads owner, principal, and status from an operator-written registry rather than from the accounts; and it accepts policy signatures from EOA owners only (`ARCHITECTURE.md` section 16.5).
- No production incident exists for the selection-specific attack surface this project is building; the mitigations in section 9's layer 3 and `EVOLUTION.md`'s protocol-immutable parameters are the best defense this research phase could ground in evidence, not a proven-in-production control.
- The funding controls in section 7 are specified, not built. Two of their premises are unverified: that a relayed EIP-3009 `transferWithAuthorization` succeeds on Monad's USDC contract (the contract exposes the type hashes; no call has been executed), and that the on-ramp vendor's destination lock cannot be changed by the user inside the widget. The vendor holds the user's money between card payment and delivery; a vendor failure in that window is outside this system's control, and is visible to the user only through the vendor's own status.
- This document does not model a fully adversarial platform operator; the operator is trusted for the MVP, per `THREAT-MODEL.md`'s stated non-goals.
- The MVP's provider allowlist (section 5) closes off x402 discovery-layer risk by narrowing scope, not by building a robust discovery-security layer; expanding the provider list in the future re-opens that surface and requires revisiting this section before it happens, not after.
- Kuru's router sits on the same capital-moving allowlist as the reallocation contract and the settlement asset (section 1). As of 2026-09-22, Kuru's own documentation says its core contracts are audited and links Spearbit and Cantina reports, plus post-fix reviews and a Kuru Flow review (`https://docs.kuru.io/contracts/Audits`). This repository has not read those reports, and has not checked that the allowlisted bytecode matches what those reports covered. CertiK's public project page, checked the same day, still lists no third-party audit. Until the reports are read and matched to the contracts this system would actually call, Kuru's audit status is unconfirmed for our purposes, and that is a residual risk of the allowlist model.
